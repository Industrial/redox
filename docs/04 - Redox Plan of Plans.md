# Redox — Plan of Plans

Status: active index
Tracks which plans, taken together, implement `docs/01 - Redox.md` as corrected and
extended by `docs/02 - Redox Update Blueprint.md` and `docs/03 - Redox Surface Design.md`.

**This document is an index only.** It does not author plan content — each row becomes its
own plan document, `docs/<doc> - <plan> - <Name>.md`, authored when its turn comes. Update the
**Status** column here as each plan is authored, executed and shipped; do not let plan
detail leak back into this file.

Convention adopted from Emerald's `history/2026-09-08T174011Z-plan-of-plans.md`, including
the three habits that make it worth keeping (doc 02 §3.1):

1. **Index only.** Scope is one cell, not a section.
2. **Correct stale labels in place**, with a date and the evidence that corrected them.
3. **Never delete a superseded row.** Mark it `superseded` and name what replaced it. The
   record of what was tried and abandoned is more valuable than a tidy list.

## Status legend

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `not-started` | Row exists, no plan authored yet |
| `planned` | Plan document authored under `docs/<doc> - <plan> - …`, not yet executed |
| `in-progress` | Executing |
| `partial` | Some of the row shipped, the rest named in its own cell |
| `done` | Shipped, gate green, reviewed |
| `superseded` | Replaced; row kept with a pointer to its successor |

`partial` was added when plan 13 landed doc 18, because that one plan delivered pieces of
sixteen later rows and *deleted* pieces of several more. Calling such a row `not-started`
would have been false and calling it `done` worse, and the alternative — splitting each row in
two — would have renumbered the register, which rule 2 forbids. A `partial` row says in its own
cell what shipped, what was deleted, and what is outstanding; nothing else is allowed to be
vague about it.

### What plan 13 did to this table

Doc 18 is a surface, not a feature, so it crossed the register rather than occupying one row.
Nineteen rows moved: **eight to `done`** (17, 24, 25, 26, 27, 31, 35, 36), **three to
`superseded`** (19, 32, 37), and **eight to `partial`** (14, 18, 21, 22, 28, 29, 30, 38). Every
one of them names what changed in its own cell.

The three `superseded` rows are worth reading together, because each was retired by *deletion*
rather than replacement: doc 18 removed the construct that needed the plan. Plan 19's eight
call-grammar rules dissolved into juxtaposition, plan 32 had nothing left to broadcast over
once `.(` and the `.+`-family left, and plan 37's `defer` closed doc 03 §23 item 1 — the one
disclosed exception to doc 01 §36 — by ceasing to exist.

`await` and nothing else — doc 18 §3.1 had given it four jobs and kept Haskell's meaning in
only one of them. This note is here so a reader of a row below is not surprised by a spelling
the row does not mention.

function. Row 39 shipped the marker and `examples/async.sdx` on **2026-11-09**, and that
example brings its own nine-line executor — doc 03 §14.3 provides none, which is the claim
the example exists to demonstrate.

**`docs/05 - 01 - Vertical Slice.md` is the authored exemplar** — every subsequent plan
follows its shape:

| § | Section | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What this plan is | Why this scope, and why not larger or smaller |
| 2 | Verified preconditions | Anything measured rather than assumed, with the command and its output |
| 3 | Concrete proof | The executable acceptance test, with the exact expected output |
| 4 | Leaves | One subsection per unit of work, implementable without further design |
| 5 | Acceptance criteria | Numbered, each independently checkable |
| 6 | Out of scope | Every deferred item named, with the successor plan that owns it |
| 7 | Gate | The commands that must pass |
| 8 | Emerald reuse | What was lifted, what was consulted, what was deliberately not taken and why |
| 9 | Decision log | Choices *and their reasons*, including the ones taken against a prior |

Two conventions worth stating because they are what make the format useful rather than
ceremonial. **§2 exists so that contingencies get removed rather than carried** — plan 01's
§2 settled doc 01 §1.1's open question about rustc's input-extension handling with a single
probe. And **§6 may not say "later"**: a deferred item without a named successor plan is a
gap, not a decision, which is Emerald's "nothing is left open without a named place it gets
resolved" rule (doc 02 §3.3).

The `From Emerald` column below is load-bearing, not decorative. Doc 02 §2 found that of
Emerald's ~107k lines the Redox-relevant surface is roughly 6.5k — `emerald-driver`,
`emerald-lsp`, `emerald-fmt`'s goal and the fuzz harness — plus `emerald-mcp`'s 392 lines,
which doc 02 §9.3 held outside that figure as optional and plan 50 now takes, plus process
and grammar methodology. A row claiming reuse must name the file; a row claiming none says
`—`, and that is the expected answer for most of the front end, because Redox's lexer is
indentation-sensitive and its parser is hand-written where Emerald's are neither.

### Sequencing, and why it is not negotiable

Three constraints, each with a reason:

1. **Plan 51 goes first.** This milestone adds roughly a dozen constructs and retires several the
   corpus depends on. A coverage gate installed *before* that work makes each row prove its own
   discoverability as a condition of being `done`, rather than leaving a dozen `$`-shaped holes.
2. **Plans 56 → 57 → 58 are a chain, and ship separately.** They could be one row. Separating
   them means the corpus migrates once for method syntax and once for pipeline semantics, each
   with its own unchanged-stdout assertion — so if the second goes wrong the first is green.
3. **Plan 64 goes last.** It deletes `print`, which every one of the 29 examples uses. Nothing
   else should be in flight when 29 files change their most common line.

Plan 59 is also constrained: it must land *after* plan 56, because currying is what turns a bare
`block` from a rustc error into a silently-passed function item. The milestone creates that
hazard and closes it in the same milestone.

## Ordering commitments

Five constraints on reordering, each with a reason that cost somebody something:

1. **Spans and trivia land in plan 01, not later.** Emerald's plan 22 found `TypeExpr`
   carried no span twenty-one plans after the diagnostics plan, and the full fix was by then
   "judged too large/risky" — so it shipped a scoped workaround and a disclosed residual gap.
2. **Milestone 2 precedes Milestone 5.** Retrofitting a source map onto an emitter not built
   for one is the same class of rework, and doc 03's multi-line expansions (`use`, `defer`,
   `with`, multi-clause merging) widen the source-to-generated gap considerably.
3. **Milestone 4 can interleave anywhere after plan 03.** Its rows are corrections to a
   published specification, not features, and two of them change what existing source means.
4. **Plan 34 should not drift late.** The formatter is the migration tool (doc 02 §4.5), and
   Emerald's plan 71 cutover missed 113 fixtures embedded as string literals in Rust test

   tests reference files.**

## Gate

Every plan clears, before it is `done`:

```bash
moon run :check          # per-crate `cargo check` — rustc's verdict
moon run :lint           # cargo fmt --check && cargo clippy -D warnings
moon run :test           # cargo nextest run, then the doctests
moon run :format         # treefmt
```

**Corrected 2026-11-10.** This block listed `:cargo-check` and `:cargo-test` until plan 51
copied it into fourteen new plans. Those targets **do not exist**: AGENTS.md records that
*"`:check` and `:test` *are* rustc's answer now"* — moon runs cargo per crate, so the
double-check they performed against blaze has no second verdict to reconcile. Plan 45 §10
recorded the sharper reason they went: an inputs-only override of a removed task resolves to
`Command: noop`, and `moon run :cargo-test` **reported success having run nothing**. A gate
naming a target that does not exist is the same defect one document up.

From Milestone 1 onward, additionally: every example `.sdx` transpiles, **its generated Rust
compiles under real rustc**, runs, and its stdout is asserted. Golden text proves stability;
compilation proves correctness; neither substitutes for the other (doc 02 §8.1–8.2).

not how many sites. They sort four ways:

- **Reachable today, awkwardly — about 109 sites.** All 61 inline test modules, because
  item-level `test "…"` already emits `#[cfg(test)] mod __rdx_tests { use super::*; }` and

  `vec![x; n]` and method-as-value workarounds. Reachable is not the same as fine: 109

- **Three parser rows the language wants anyway** — `for` over a pattern, open-ended ranges,

  for `matches!`'s 61 sites — otherwise `case` ladders and a `match_like_matches_macro`
  allow in seven crates. And `as`, 29 sites, where the nearest working thing is `try_from`

- **One genuinely hard case.** `thread_local!`, a macro whose body is a **declaration**, which

  it in a port, so that crate needs the macro form or a stated exemption.

**And 100% cannot mean deleting the Rust.** Doc 02 §4.8 keeps the Rust implementation
*permanently*, correcting doc 01 §34. The coherent target is what row 68 did once: every
crate has a `.sdx` source and the committed `.rs` is generated — both representations, with
a regeneration test, forever. That is a standing cost, and the yield each port reports is
what it has to be weighed against.

**Amended 2026-11-10 by row 72, on a narrower argument than a change of mind.** The
paragraph above weighed two shapes — a `build.rs`, which is a dependency cycle cargo
rejects, and committed output — and called the second *"the only shape available"*. There
is a third it did not consider. A build script must be **compiled from the workspace being
built**, which is what makes the cycle; a **pre-built artifact is an input**, like rustc
itself, and there is no cycle to reject. So row 72 seeds a checked-in `redox1.wasm` from
the Rust that exists today, and each port after it untracks its crate's generated `.rs`
rather than committing it.

Two things that paragraph got right survive intact. The committed `.rs` **is** a better
stage0 than a downloaded or blobbed one *while the port is partial* — it is readable, and
plan 70 §1 is the survey showing nobody else can have that. And the per-crate flip matters
for exactly that reason: while a crate's Rust is hand-written it **is** the source, so the
ignore is the last step of each port and never a one-time switch.

What changes at the end is who the committed tree is *for*. At two of twelve ported it is
239 KB a reviewer might read; at twelve of twelve it is ~32,000 lines of output with no
author, which nobody reviews because the `.sdx` is the thing to review. The bootstrap is
then the only argument left for keeping it, and the seed discharges the bootstrap.

**One decision is owed to a human and row 72 does not take it.** Doc 02 §4.8's *"keep the
Rust implementation permanently"* would be satisfied in **letter** by what remains —
18,585 lines of tests, which plan 69 decision 5 keeps in Rust, and
`redox-driver/build.rs`, which cargo runs on the host — and **not** in the spirit its
author meant, which was a parallel hand-written compiler. Row 72 §6 states the problem;
leaf 7 asks for the reading rather than picking one.

**The test is not that a crate shipped.** It is that `cargo check --workspace` compiles
against Redox-generated Rust and all 845 tests pass — which is the assertion plan 48's port
could not make, because a module lifted out of a crate cannot resolve `crate::Stmt` and its
oracle had to be `syn::parse_file` rather than rustc.

**The Rust implementation stays permanently**, per doc 02 §4.8 correcting doc 01 §34. A
ported crate's generated `.rs` is **committed** and cargo compiles it with no `build.rs` —
not timidity, but the only shape available: `compile_dir` lives in `redox-driver`, which

## Milestone 11 — Expressive delta

**Added 2026-11-11, after the surface was assessed from outside.** Milestone 9 asked whether the
surface is *coherent* and milestone 10 whether it is *sufficient to compile itself*. Both closed
`done`. This milestone asks the question neither of them does: **does the surface buy a capability,
or only a notation?**

The measurement that prompted it is unflattering and worth keeping. Scored on fifteen axes from
the README alone, Redox rates **9/10 on semantic transparency** — the generated Rust is the Rust a
person would have written — **9/10 on verification discipline**, and **3/10 on expressive delta
over Rust**, because every construct in the surface is a spelling for something Rust can already
say. Rust has algebraic data types, exhaustive matching, iterator chains and trait-based
abstraction; what Redox adds is layout, equations, guards, sections, `$` and `|>`. That is real
ergonomics and it is not new capability.

The constraint is what makes the milestone interesting rather than impossible. Doc 02 §12.4
forbids a type checker and `docs/DECLINED.md` holds the line, so the question is sharp:

> **What capability can a type-free source-to-source transform add to Rust, with rustc still the
> sole authority on types and borrows?**

It has better answers than the register had taken, and the reason is an asymmetry: a syntactic
transform can add a great deal as long as what it adds concerns **code generation** rather than
**type reasoning**. Rust's own answer to code generation is the proc macro, and a proc macro cannot
change the grammar, cannot participate in layout, cannot see a sibling item, and needs its own
crate. Every row below sits in that gap.

### Sequencing

84 and 85 first, and in that order: both are local, both close boundaries the register already
records, and 85 depends on nothing 84 changes but reads better after it. 86 next — it is the
largest expressiveness gain in the milestone and touches one arm. 87 and 88 are independent of
each other. 89 depends on 87 for its expansion machinery. **90 is last**, because it is the only
row that changes the lexer, and the lexer is what every other row's surface is read by.

**Corrected 2026-11-11, after 90 shipped.** The sequencing was right and the reason was
overstated: 90 changed the lexer by **six lines** — one more `const` run tried before
`OPERATOR_TABLE` — not by the pre-pass over raw source its §1.3 priced. That cost followed
from an assumption §1 never made, that an author picks the *spelling*; a closed set of twelve
keeps every property the row wanted and discards only the freedom to invent a glyph. Plan 90
§10.1 is the finding, and it generalises: **a cost estimate inherits the assumptions of the
design it was made against, and the design is the thing to question first.**

### What this milestone does not claim

It does not reach 10/10 on the axis it is named for, and should not try. A score that high requires
inference, higher-kinded abstraction or effect tracking, each of which needs the checker doc 02
§12.4 forbids — so the honest ceiling under the constraint is **8–9**, and the constraint is worth
more than the tenth point. Doc 02 §12.4 stays.

One re-cut is worth considering and is **not** taken by any row here, so it is recorded as an open
question rather than assumed: the constraint is stated broadly enough to read as forbidding all
static analysis, where what it means to forbid is a *type* checker and a *borrow* checker. Redox
already resolves names, arities, operator fixity and pattern expansion, and those failures are the
only ones a transpiler can honestly own. Rows 85, 86, 87, 89 and 90 each carry a diagnostic of that
kind, and each argues for it locally. Whether the corollary should say so in general is a question
for a row that owns the diagnostics surface, not for any of these.
